METR's 80% data fits an exponential almost as well as a logistic

Take METR's published 80%-success horizons for every public model it tested since 2024 and fit both curves. A logistic curve that levels off near fits better than the exponential, and the newest models sit on the flat part of it. The exponential is the runner-up, not the obvious reading. UNVERIFIED Claude Mythos Preview (early): why it is left out of the fit and hidden by default

  • Unreleased snapshot. METR tested an early pre-release version of Mythos during a limited window in March 2026 (The Decoder). The public never had that version, so no one outside METR and Anthropic can re-run it.
  • METR's own warning. On its chart METR says "Measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite" (METR). METR's 50% estimate for this snapshot is about 17 hours, and only 5 of the 228 tasks are 16 hours or longer. Its 80% interval, 1.6 to 6.6 hours, is the widest of any model here.
  • Independence questions. METR writes that "it has proved challenging to ensure that we have adequate independence" (METR). It relies on the labs it assesses for access and free tokens. Critics have also traced funding ties back to Anthropic investors; those specific figures haven't been independently verified (OfficeChai).
  • Why it matters here. This one point carries much of the exponential's case. With it included, the logistic's edge on METR's numbers drops from +5.6 to +1.5 AIC (see the last column of the data-options table).

What the fit shows

01

On METR's own numbers, the logistic fits better. Across the public models since 2024, the logistic beats the exponential by AIC points after paying for its extra parameter. That makes the exponential about as likely.

02

The newest models sit on the flat part. The curve passed its halfway point around . Since then most releases have landed between 50 minutes and 1.5 hours at 80% success, under the exponential line (chart below).

03

Every pessimistic adjustment strengthens the case. Using the low end of METR's error bars, or scaling down long-task human times 2x or 5x, keeps the logistic ahead. With 5x the exponential is about as likely.

04

The two readings point to very different 2028s. The logistic holds the 80% horizon near into 2028. The exponential fitted to the same points would need it to reach by January 2028.

Where the exponential misses

Each dot shows how far one model lands from a fitted curve: above the line means the model did better than the curve predicts, below means worse. A good fit leaves the dots scattered evenly around 1x.

The exponential's misses follow a pattern. The early-2024 models fall below it, the 2025 models rise above it, and the 2026 models drop below it again. That under-over-under run is what a straight line leaves behind when it is forced through data that bends. The logistic's misses are smaller and show no such run.

Where the long-task times come from

Today's 80% horizons sit around one to three hours, but their fits lean on tasks rated at 4 to 30 hours. Those are the thinnest and least measured part of METR's suite.

METR says some tasks have estimated times rather than measured ones, and that most of the long ones lack baselines. The task file doesn't say which, so the table uses a rough check: a time on an exact multiple of 30 minutes (4 h, 8 h, 16 h) is unlikely to be an average of measured runs. Treat it as a hint only.

The baselines came from people recruited mainly through METR staff's professional networks, paid hourly plus bonuses. In METR's own test, contractors took 5–18x longer than repo maintainers. If long-task times run long, the AI horizons built on them run long too.

Method

Data. METR's Time Horizon 1.1 results (26 models) and task-level results (23 models, up to 228 tasks), downloaded from metr.org on 14 November 2026. To check the code, METR's per-model regression was re-run; it reproduces every published 80% horizon to within .

Fits. Both curves are fitted to log₂(80% horizon) against release date. Each model is weighted by its 95% interval. The exponential is a straight line in log space. The logistic is log₂h = log₂K − log₂(1 + e−r(t−t₀)): it rises like an exponential early on, then levels off at a ceiling K. Written out in minutes, the two fits are h = A·ek(t−2024) and h = K / (1 + e−r(t−t₀)), with t in calendar years; the fitted values for each data option appear beside the chart. AIC charges the logistic for its third parameter. The errors are scaled so the exponential's reduced χ² equals 1.

Long-task options. Human times are divided by a factor that is 1 at one hour and under, rises evenly on a log scale, and reaches 2x or 5x at 16 hours and above. Each model's horizon is then refitted from METR's task data. These are assumptions, not measurements.

How much the choices matter. In total, combinations were tried of start year (2019–2025), record-setters vs. all models, Mythos in or out, the four data options and two weightings. The logistic fits better in of them, and by more than 2 AIC points in . Those are mainly the 2024-onward windows used here. With METR's own convention (record-setters, 2019 onward, Mythos included) the exponential is ahead by AIC points. The page shows the strongest case for the logistic: models from January 2024 on, every public model rather than only record-setters, the unreleased Mythos Preview left out, and each model weighted by METR's own 95% interval.
