The Erdős unit distance problem

Posed in 1946 and disproved in 2026, though only for unimaginably large numbers of points (YouTube video).

The problem

Put n points anywhere on a flat plane. Some pairs of them may be exactly one unit apart. If you arrange the points as cleverly as possible, how many such pairs can you get? Call that maximum u(n). The question is how fast u(n) grows as n gets large.

A few easy facts set the range. Points spaced along a line give n − 1 pairs, so the count is at least linear. It cannot be quadratic either. Two unit circles meet in at most two points, and that limits how tangled the "distance 1" connections can get. In 1984, Spencer, Szemerédi and Trotter proved the best known ceiling: at most a constant times n4/3 pairs.

Erdős's own idea for a good arrangement was a square grid, scaled carefully. In a grid, the distance between two points is √k for some whole number k. Some numbers can be written as a sum of two squares in many ways (65 = 1² + 8² = 4² + 7²). If you pick such a k and rescale the grid so that √k becomes the unit, each point gets many unit-distance neighbours at once. That gives

u(n) ≥ n1 + c / log log nErdős's grid: barely faster than linear, and the extra exponent slowly shrinks toward zero

Erdős conjectured that this was essentially the best possible: u(n) = n1+o(1). That means no arrangement beats linear growth by any fixed power of n. For eighty years nobody found a counterexample, and most people believed he was right.

The solution

In May 2026, an internal OpenAI reasoning model produced a proof that the conjecture is false. Mathematicians then checked and simplified it. The construction swaps the ordinary integers behind the grid for the integers of high-degree algebraic number fields. It uses infinite class field towers and the Golod-Shafarevich theorem to find fields that create many unit distances at once. Will Sawin then made the exponent explicit:

n1.014  ≲  u(n)  ≲  n4/3For infinitely many n. The lower bound is a fixed power above 1, so Erdős's conjecture fails.

The catch

A fixed exponent of 1.014 always beats an exponent that tends to 1, but only in the limit. The grid's exponent is about 1 + ln 2 / ln ln n. It shrinks extremely slowly, because it depends on the log of a log of n. To find where the new construction overtakes the grid, set the two extra exponents equal:

ln 2 / ln ln n = 0.014  ⟹  ln ln n ≈ 49  ⟹  n ≈ 101021Leading-order estimate

If you compute the grid's best count exactly, instead of using only the leading term, the crossover moves slightly later:

Estimated crossover
n ≈ 101.3 × 1021
Above this many points, the new construction gives more unit-distance pairs than Erdős's grid, and it stays ahead forever.

This is not 1021 points. It is a number whose count of digits is about 1.3 × 1021, which is 1.3 sextillion digits. Written out at one millimetre per digit, it would stretch about 135 light-years. For comparison, the observable universe holds roughly 1080 atoms, a number with only 81 digits.

So the result is purely asymptotic. It proves that Erdős's intuition about how the count grows was wrong. It changes nothing about the arrangements anyone could ever draw, store or compute: at every physically meaningful scale, the humble grid still wins.

How the crossover was estimated. The grid gives about n · r₂(k) pairs, where k ≤ n is the squared unit length and r₂(k) counts the ways to write k as a sum of two squares. The best k is a product of many small primes of the form 4m + 1, with exponents chosen the way highly composite numbers are built. Prime counts come from the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions. This model was checked against exact computer searches up to 1016; the table values for 1010 and 1080 come from exact searches, the rest from the model.

Constant factors, including the unspecified constant in Sawin's theorem, don't matter at this scale. With ln n ≈ 3 × 1021, even a factor of 10100 shifts the crossover by a negligible amount. The exponent is what matters. 1.014 is a proven lower bound, so the construction may actually do better. An unreviewed preprint reports an exponent of about 1.0316; if it holds, the crossover drops to roughly 102 × 109, which is still far beyond anything physical. The construction also only exists for special values of n.
