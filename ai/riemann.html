The Riemann Hypothesis (1859), from the ground up

The hypothesis says every non-trivial zero of the zeta function has real part ½. The sections below explain each part of that statement, with interactive figures. (YouTube video)

1. Complex numbers

The equation x² = −1 has no solution among the real numbers. The imaginary unit i is defined by i² = −1. Descartes coined the word "imaginary" in 1637; Euler introduced the symbol i.

A complex number has the form s = σ + ti, where σ and t are real numbers:

  • σ = Re(s) is the real part.
  • t = Im(s) is the imaginary part.

Since a complex number is determined by two real numbers, it can be plotted as a point in the complex plane: real part on the horizontal axis, imaginary part on the vertical axis.

The zero at ½ + 14.13i has real part ½ and imaginary part 14.13. "Real part ½" fixes the horizontal coordinate only; the imaginary part is unconstrained. Drag the point to read both coordinates. It snaps onto an axis when you drag close to one; hold Alt to drag freely.

2. The zeta function

The Riemann zeta function assigns to each complex number s a complex value ζ(s). Where Re(s) > 1 it is defined by the series:

ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + 1/5s + …

Try it with s = 2: 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + 1/16 + … The terms shrink fast and the total settles near 1.6449, a number Euler identified in 1734 as π²/6. Slide to add more terms.

In 1737 Euler showed that, for Re(s) > 1, the series equals a product with one factor for each prime. This Euler product is why the behaviour of ζ carries information about the primes.

ζ(s) = 1/(1 − 2−s) × 1/(1 − 3−s) × 1/(1 − 5−s) × …

Beyond the series: analytic continuation

The series converges only for Re(s) > 1. At s = 1 it is the harmonic series 1 + ½ + ⅓ + …, which diverges; for Re(s) ≤ 1 the series does not converge.

An analytic (complex-differentiable) function is determined everywhere by its values on any small region, so it has at most one analytic extension to a larger domain. In 1859 Riemann showed that ζ extends to every complex s except s = 1, where it has a simple pole. This extension, the analytic continuation, is what "the zeta function" means from here on, and it is what this page computes.

A zero of ζ is a value of s with ζ(s) = 0. The Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about where the zeros lie.

4. Why exactly ½?

The functional equation

Riemann proved that the completed zeta function

ξ(s) = ½ s(s − 1) π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s)

satisfies ξ(s) = ξ(1 − s). The zeros of ξ are exactly the non-trivial zeros of ζ, so if ρ is a non-trivial zero, so is 1 − ρ. Because ζ is real on the real axis, its zeros are also symmetric under complex conjugation (ρ and ρ̄). Together, the non-trivial zeros are symmetric about the line Re(s) = ½.

A zero with real part 0.7 would therefore force one with real part 0.3 at the same height. A zero coincides with its mirror image exactly when its real part is ½.

The hypothesis states that every non-trivial zero has real part ½. Known results: no zeros lie on Re(s) = 1 (Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, 1896, which gave the prime number theorem); infinitely many lie on the critical line (Hardy, 1914); more than 40% do (Conrey, 1989). Whether all do is open.

The image of a vertical line

Fix the real part σ and let the imaginary part t run from 0 to T. The curve is the image of that vertical segment under ζ, drawn in the output plane. For σ = ½ it passes through 0 whenever t is the imaginary part of a zero. For any other σ it does not, because every zero with imaginary part below 50 lies on the critical line.

5. What it tells us about primes, and what it doesn't

What it does not do

  • It does not generate primes. It gives no formula for the n-th prime and no faster way to produce primes.
  • It does not test whether a number is prime. The generalized hypothesis would make Miller's 1976 primality test provably correct. But the AKS test (2002) already decides primality in polynomial time with no unproven assumption, and practical software uses fast probabilistic tests.
  • It does not factor numbers, so it has no bearing on RSA encryption, whose security rests on factoring being hard.

What it does

  • It says how accurately we can predict how many primes there are. The prime number theorem says the number of primes up to x, π(x), is approximately the logarithmic integral li(x). The hypothesis is equivalent to the error being at most about √x ln x (von Koch, 1901). Schoenfeld (1976) made this explicit: if the hypothesis holds, |π(x) − li(x)| < √x ln x / (8π) for all x ≥ 2657. Without it, the best proven error bounds are far weaker.
  • It limits the gaps between primes. Assuming it, Cramér (1920) proved that for large x there is always a prime between x and x + C √x ln x, for some constant C. Without it, the best proven result is a prime between x and x + x0.525 (Baker, Harman and Pintz, 2001).
  • It underpins other theorems. Many results in number theory are proved only by assuming it; a proof makes them all unconditional.

The estimate: li(x)

Around 1792 Gauss observed that near a number t, roughly 1 in ln t numbers is prime. Adding up that density gives the logarithmic integral:

li(x) = ∫0x dt / ln t

The prime number theorem (1896) says π(x) and li(x) agree in the long run; its proof rests on ζ having no zeros on Re(s) = 1. Riemann's exact formula goes further. li(x) is the main term, coming from the pole of ζ at s = 1, and each zero ρ adds a correction li(xρ), whose size depends on the real part of ρ. If every real part is ½, the corrections stay of order √x ln x. That is why the hypothesis is equivalent to a statement about how far π(x) can stray from li(x).

The prediction, tested

Actual prime counts against the estimate li(x). Then two guarantees for how large the error can be: the one the hypothesis would give, √x ln x / (8π) (Schoenfeld, 1976), and the best one proven without it, 0.2795 x e−√(ln x / 6.455) / (ln x)3/4 (Trudgian, 2016).

6. Why it's worth $1 million

  • It was picked as one of the hardest problems in mathematics. In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute chose seven problems for the new millennium, $1 million each. Only the Poincaré Conjecture has been solved.
  • It has resisted the best minds since 1859. It was on Hilbert's famous 1900 list, and many consider it the most important open problem in pure mathematics.
  • Evidence isn't proof. Computers have checked the first 10 trillion zeros; all sit on the line. An exception could still hide further up, so only a proof settles it.
  • A great deal rests on it. Many theorems, on gaps between primes and more, are proved only by assuming it. A proof makes them all true at once.

Where the 2026 claim stands

On 20 November 2026, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge posted a note saying that, working with Claude, he believes he has proved the hypothesis (and its generalized form). The proof exists only as 17.4 million lines of computer-checked Lean code, with no paper. Independent experts report it compiles; a final cross-check was still running. By his own account he does not yet understand all of it. Clay's rules require publication, a two-year wait and community acceptance.
